Who notices when someone stops coming?

The answer is usually built into things nobody thinks of as belonging: who gets the invitation, who can physically get there, who owns the next handoff. That is the work I do.

yairwalton@gmail.com

The work

Isolation gets talked about as something that happens to a person. Most of the time it is something a system did. The schedule, the room, the ride that does or doesn't exist, the handoff nobody owns. Those are decisions, and decisions can be made differently.

Access is not the door. It is the whole path to the room, and the reason to return.

So we follow the person, not the building. Home, assisted living, skilled nursing, whatever comes next. The relationship does not get an end date because the address changed.

Then there is the counting. A number can be technically correct and still answer the wrong question. A better participation number is not proof of impact. It is a more honest place to start.

My colleagues and I have been building measures that hold two things at once: how far into a community the work reaches, and who is in the room once it gets there. More than 1,300 older adults since 2020. The number I would rather know is how many of them have somebody who would notice they were gone.

Aging is where I'm doing this right now; it isn't the whole of what interests me. The same problems turn up anywhere people move between institutions and somebody assumes belonging will sort itself out.

I'm writing about this at more length, and I'll link the pieces here once they're published. If you want to know when that happens, I can send you an email.

About

Yair Walton is Senior Rabbi and Director of Program Operations and Strategy for K'vod Connect at Jewish Family Service of Cincinnati, which has reached more than 1,300 older adults since 2020. He works on the systems that decide whether someone stays visible to a community, and on the harder problem of measuring whether they do.

He leads a multidisciplinary team spanning program management, licensed social work, chaplaincy, and data. In 2025 the program received the Outstanding Project in the Field of Aging award from the Tristate Association for Professionals in Aging.
