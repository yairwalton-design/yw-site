Who notices when someone stops coming?

The answer is usually built into things nobody thinks of as belonging: who gets the invitation, who can physically get there, who owns the next handoff. That is the work I do.

The work

Isolation gets talked about as something that happens to a person. Most of the time it is something a system did. The schedule, the room, the ride that does or doesn't exist, the handoff nobody owns. Those are decisions, and decisions can be made differently.

That holds in a company, a hospital, a school, a congregation. I am working on it in aging services right now, which is where the problem is sharpest and the stakes are plainest, but nothing about it is particular to aging.

Access is not the door. It is the whole path to the room, and the reason to return.

So we follow the person, not the building. In my current work that means staying with someone from home to assisted living to skilled nursing, so the relationship does not restart every time the address does. Somewhere else it is onboarding, or a discharge, or the fourth handoff in a process nobody has walked end to end. Same shape.

Then there is the counting. A number can be technically correct and still answer the wrong question. A better participation number is not proof of impact. It is a more honest place to start.

My colleagues and I have been building measures that hold two things at once: how far into a community the work reaches, and who is in the room once it gets there. More than 1,300 older adults since 2020. The number I would rather know is how many of them have somebody who would notice they were gone.

I'm writing about this at more length, and I'll link the pieces here once they're published. If you want to know when that happens, I can send you an email.

Bio

Yair Walton builds programs and the operating systems underneath them: partnership networks, outcome measurement, data infrastructure, and the teams that hold all of it together. He works on the systems that decide whether a person stays visible to an organization, and on the harder problem of measuring whether they do.

He is Senior Rabbi and Director of Program Operations and Strategy for K'vod Connect at Jewish Family Service of Cincinnati, which has reached more than 1,300 older adults since 2020, and he leads a multidisciplinary team spanning program management, licensed social work, chaplaincy, and data. In 2025 the program received the Outstanding Project in the Field of Aging award from the Tristate Association for Professionals in Aging.

He was ordained at Hebrew Union College in 2021. The training shows up less in ritual than in method: how he listens, what he asks, and a stubbornness about not letting individual people disappear inside a large system.

He lives in Cincinnati with his wife and their young daughter. Outside work he camps, explores new places with them, tinkers with emerging technology, and has spent an unreasonable amount of time on the surprisingly difficult problem of making restaurant quality noodles at home.
