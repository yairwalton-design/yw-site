What keeps someone connected after the welcome is over?

I spend my days on that question and on the unglamorous work sitting under it: who gets a ride, and who notices when somebody stops showing up.

yairwalton@gmail.com

The work

Isolation usually gets talked about as something that happens to a person. I've come to think it's closer to a byproduct of how we build things: the schedule, the room, the ride that does or doesn't exist, the handoff between one program and the next. Those are design decisions, and a decision can be made differently.

The hardest moment to stay connected to people is usually the moment they move.

So we try to follow them through it. Home, assisted living, skilled nursing, whatever comes next; the relationship doesn't get an end date just because the address changed.

The other half of this is measurement, and it's genuinely hard; I don't think any of us have it solved, myself included. A bigger attendance number isn't proof that anybody is less alone, but it's a more honest place to start asking.

My colleagues and I have been building measures that hold two things at once: how far into a community the work actually reaches, and who is in the room once it gets there. More than 1,300 older adults since 2020, and the number I'd rather know is how many of them have somebody who would notice they were gone.

Aging is where I'm doing this right now; it isn't the whole of what interests me. The same problems turn up anywhere people move between institutions and somebody assumes belonging will sort itself out.

I'm writing about this at more length, and I'll link the pieces here once they're published.

About

Yair Walton is Senior Rabbi and Director of Program Operations and Strategy for K'vod Connect at Jewish Family Service of Cincinnati, which has reached more than 1,300 older adults since 2020. He works on the systems that decide whether someone stays visible to a community, and on the harder problem of measuring whether they do.

He leads a multidisciplinary team spanning program management, licensed social work, chaplaincy, and data. In 2025 the program received the Outstanding Project in the Field of Aging award from the Tristate Association for Professionals in Aging.
